Root causeA national retailer
51.5% drop, relabeled

Organic search revenue was not lost. It was filed under Direct.

A site change made organic revenue fall 51.5% in two weeks. The money had moved, not disappeared.

Question. Did we lose $0.8M of organic search revenue after a site change, or did it move?
Method. 14-day windows before and after the change, the same dates last year as a control, app traffic as a second control, and every segment cut by device, operating system, and browser. 24M sessions in BigQuery.
Result. Every organic segment fell about the same, Direct nearly doubled, and total web revenue was down 1.3% against last year's 3.1%. The referrer from Google was being lost in the browser before GA4 ran, so visits arrived with no source.
What it does not mean. Nothing was recovered or saved. The revenue was never lost. The win is the correct read, which stopped a wrong decision.
How I checked it. A second pull matched the first within 0.2% on every row. Organic matched the GA4 interface to the cent. The client's manager forwarded it to three teams.

$2.7M to $5.9M

What iOS deep linking in paid search is really worth.

The estimated annual incremental revenue, as a range, and the reason it is a range. It reshaped the channel budget.

Question. Does deep linking add revenue, or does it take credit for sales that organic would have made anyway?
Method. A quasi-experimental comparison in GA4 and BigQuery that separates real lift from organic cannibalization, with the assumptions written down next to the numbers.
Result. An estimated $2.7M to $5.9M a year in incremental revenue. Presented to senior stakeholders, who changed the channel budget.
What it does not mean. It is an estimate and a range on purpose. A single figure would have hidden how much depends on the cannibalization assumption.
How I checked it. The math is rebuildable from the raw tables, and I can walk through every step on a call.

Hypothesis testA national retailer
dry +17%, wet −8%

Why Memorial Day weekend sales fell: the weather.

Fifty-one states split into dry, flat, and wet by the change in rainfall. The drier the state, the better the weekend.

Question. Was the holiday weekend drop a marketing problem or a weather problem?
Method. Grouped state-level sales by rainfall change versus last year: dry (less rain, 8 states), flat (within 0.05 inch, 11 states), wet (more rain, 32 states). Tested the groups against each other, then re-tested at finer cuts to make sure the order held.
Result. Dry states rose 17.2%, flat states held at +0.9%, wet states fell 8.2%. Presented visually to the client's CMO.
What it does not mean. Weather explains the split between states. It does not explain every dollar of the total, and the analysis says so.
How I checked it. Re-tested at finer rainfall cuts before presenting. Same order, same direction.

Matched controlA national retailer
$3.15M

More quarterly revenue in centralized-fulfillment stores than in matched stores.

455 stores against standalone stores matched on state and size. Nearly equal traffic, 18.4% higher conversion.

Question. Do stores on centralized fulfillment convert better than comparable standalone stores?
Method. Matched 455 stores to standalone stores on state and store size across the top 20 states, 20.8M sessions in BigQuery, matched on users rather than sessions because sessions are partly a result of the treatment. Mann-Whitney per store in Python.
Result. Session conversion 1.21% vs 1.03%, revenue per session $1.95 vs $1.64, average order value equal. A $3.15M gap in one quarter.
What it does not mean. A gap between matched groups, not proof of cause, and never an annual number. Part could be store traits the match did not capture.
How I checked it. Caught a store-ID bug first: zero-padded IDs had put 19 treated stores in the control group. Fixed the key and added a check that must return zero rows.

Flagship buildA global luxury retailer · APAC
4 countries, 1 source of truth

The real-time APAC dashboard regional leadership runs on.

An end-to-end GA4-to-Power BI pipeline on the intraday export: eight fact tables and 100+ DAX measures behind five pages, built e-commerce-ready before the sites transacted.

Question. Can every APAC market report the same numbers from the same place, before and after e-commerce goes live?
Method. Session-level spine and star schema in BigQuery, eight fact tables plus six dimensions, two scheduled queries, 100+ DAX measures with a full year-over-year layer, and a self-serve trends page where the user picks the KPI and the breakdown.
Result. Four countries report from one place. Page one is the top KPIs per site for regional leadership. Pages two to four are channel, product, sessions, users. Purchase and funnel reporting turned on at launch with no rebuild.
What it does not mean. One source of truth is a habit, not a file. It holds only while every market reads the dashboard instead of running its own pulls, which is why the training program exists.
How I checked it. A 12-check SQL QC suite that must pass before any load ships: grain integrity, fact-to-spine reconciliation, year-over-year balance, channel coverage. One catch on the way: engaged sessions undercounted one market's users by 75% to 87%, so the user-ID path was validated at zero mismatches over seven days.

Data qualityA national retailer
4% shown, 1% real

A dashboard overstating unavailable products four times over.

Ten tables behind a six-page dashboard. Three frozen, seven missing 21 days, and a mobile bug marking every app view unavailable.

Question. Why does the availability dashboard say 4% of product views are unavailable for pickup when the stores say otherwise?
Method. Audited all ten source tables: job schedules, lookback windows, data types. Apps sent the availability flag as a number, web sent it as text, so every mobile view had been marked unavailable since March.
Result. Filled 21 missing days, changed four scheduled queries to a three-day lookback with a duplicate guard, and sized the backfill at about 300 GB instead of the first estimate of 2 TB.
What it does not mean. The year-to-date rebuild is running; the final corrected rate is not on record yet.
How I checked it. Each fixed table reconciled against the raw GA4 export for a test window before the scheduled queries were changed.

Data qualityA luxury client
767K bot sessions

Twenty percent of US traffic was a datacenter in Virginia.

Hiding in the Direct channel. Found through GA4 engagement and geo signatures. The exclusion segment restored the true conversion rate.

Question. Why did Direct become the number one channel overnight, and is the conversion rate real?
Method. Profiled sessions by engagement time, source, landing page, and city. 767K of 3.94M US sessions came from one datacenter city with about zero seconds of engagement, no source, and 97% landing on the store locator.
Result. With the exclusion, conversion rate went from 0.7% to 0.9%, average engagement from 27 to 34 seconds, and Paid Social became the true top channel. Two GA4 segments shipped to the client.
What it does not mean. Revenue barely changed (down 1.4%). The bots inflated volume, not sales. The fix is permanent only at the CDN level, which was recommended.
How I checked it. Showed the remaining surge was legitimate: other sources scaled in proportion, state mix stayed stable, organic doubled with healthy engagement.

MonitoringA global beauty group
9 sites, every hour

An hourly tracking monitor that compares each site to itself.

Baselines from yesterday, the same weekday last week, and a three-day average, all cut to the same complete hours so a partial day never looks like a drop.

Question. How do nine e-commerce sites get watched for tracking breaks without a person refreshing dashboards?
Method. One raw scan per hour feeds two hourly tables and views in BigQuery, 28 days kept, surfaced in Looker Studio. A problem is a drop of 20% or more against the three-day average when last week agrees.
Result. Running hourly. It has helped catch bot-traffic inflation once or twice. A deployment runbook makes it reusable for other clients.
What it does not mean. Hourly, not real time. Low-volume sites needed tuned rules to stop false alarms on add-to-cart.
How I checked it. A guard stops a thin refresh from overwriting good data.

Root causeA national retailer
4 months frozen

A table every job said was fine, and was not.

Five pipeline layers traced to one missing extraction script. Every scheduled job had reported success on empty input.

Question. Why did a store-level table a pricing model depends on stop in February while every job showed green?
Method. Walked the pipeline layer by layer through job history and metadata until the input was empty: the Google Ads API extraction script had vanished from the storage bucket after a redeployment.
Result. Script restored, data backfilled through July, confirmed by the client's engineer.
What it does not mean. A green job means the job ran, not that data arrived. That is now a check, not an assumption.
How I checked it. Row counts per layer per day, compared before and after the restore.

How I work

Control first, then the number.

Start from the business question

"Did we lose revenue, or did it move?" The data comes second.

Find a control before trusting a number

The same dates last year, another channel, app traffic, a same-host test. Then cut by segment to rule things out.

Reconcile to the source

Every figure is checked against the GA4 interface or the raw event tables before it goes out. To the cent when possible.

Say what a number does not mean

A range with the reason it is a range beats one false-precise figure. Name what cannot be separated yet.

Make the reader self-sufficient

Send the workbook, the raw numbers, and the query they can run every week. Then train them to run it.

Build with AI, keep the judgment

I set the logic, run every query, check the result against the source, and own the outcome. The agent drafts; I validate.
