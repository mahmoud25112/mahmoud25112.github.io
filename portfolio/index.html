Case 01 · Root cause · A national retailer of outdoor equipment

Why Memorial Day sales fell

Leadership saw a 3.3% drop and asked why. I showed the drop followed the rain.

Delivered to the client's CMO, as one chart. Reception: the client loved how easy it was to read. What they did next is not known to me.

  1. The worryHoliday weekend sales were down 3.3% on last year, and nobody knew why.
  2. The ideaThis retailer sells outdoor goods, and it had rained across much of the country.
  3. The testGroup all 51 states by one thing: more rain, the same, or less rain than last year.
  4. The answerSales moved with the rain. Less rain, up 17%. More rain, down 8.2%.

The key move

I began with rain or no rain. Then I saw that a state with rain in both years is not a rainy state, it is an unchanged one. So I grouped by the change in rain, which gave me a built-in control.

What it does not mean

It does not prove rain caused the whole drop. It shows rain is large enough that the drop should not be read as a marketing failure.

Method, checks and tools
Method

GA4 sales by state, joined to public NOAA weather data, averaging the many stations in each state. Group figures are weighted by revenue.

How I checked it

If the pattern were noise, the unchanged group would not sit between the other two. I also tested temperature, which showed nothing, and re-cut the groups at finer thresholds.

Tools
BigQueryGA4NOAA public weather dataPython

Client figures anonymized: percentages are real, absolute values are left out.

Case 02 · Incrementality · A national retailer

What iOS deep linking was worth

iPhone shoppers got a shortcut from the search ad straight into the app. Android shoppers did not. That gave me a natural experiment.

Delivered to senior stakeholders, with next steps: roll the change out to Android, and run an A/B test to replace the range with one exact figure.

  1. The changeDeep linking was switched on for iOS search ads only.
  2. The comparisonSame dates a year apart, iOS against Android, which never got the change.
  3. The cautionAssume the worst: some of the gain only moved from one label to another.
  4. The answerEven then, the estimate is more than half of the iOS growth.

Why I trust it

iOS search-ad visits grew 36 times while the share of visits that bought stayed stable. If the shortcut were only re-routing people already about to buy, that share would have jumped. Web search-ad revenue also grew 58%, so nothing was taken from the website.

What it does not mean

The 34.5% is total iOS growth, not what deep linking caused. Android is a control, not a randomized test, so the honest answer is a range and not one number.

Method, checks and tools
The cautious end of the range

I assumed the rest of iOS would have grown at Android's rate without the change, and subtracted the shortfall as revenue that only changed labels.

Why a year apart

Comparing the same dates a year apart removes seasonality. GA4 was used so every traffic source was counted, not only paid.

Tools
GA4BigQueryQuasi-experimental design

Case 03 · Matched comparison · A national retailer

Shared warehouse vs standalone stores

Stores that ship from a shared warehouse can sell everything on the website. Standalone stores often show "not available in this store." I measured what that costs.

Delivered to the client, as the answer to their question: yes, these stores sell more, and the gap sits at add to cart, not at checkout.

  1. The claimStores with a shared warehouse sell more online. True, or just more items in carts?
  2. The fair fightMatch the two groups by state, store size and number of shoppers.
  3. The funnelCompare every step, from viewing a product to paying.
  4. The answerOne step differs: adding to cart, 18% higher. Everything else is level.
12 vs 10purchases per 1,000 visits, rounded (1.21% vs 1.03%, a gap of 18.4%)
+19%revenue per visit
Sameorder size, within 0.3%

Why it matters

Same browsing at the top, same checkout at the bottom, same basket size. The whole gap sits at the one step where the "not available" message appears. That is a specific, fixable loss.

What it does not mean

It is not a randomized experiment. Stores were chosen for a shared warehouse for reasons the data cannot see. It covers one peak-season quarter and should not be multiplied into a yearly figure.

Method, checks and tools
The match

The same number of stores per state across the top 20 states, a similar size profile, at least 1,000 users per store. Traffic came out within 0.4% without being matched on.

How I checked it

Tested per store, not per visit, because the stores are what differ: Mann-Whitney test, p < 0.0001 for both conversion and revenue per user. The shared-warehouse group led in all 12 complete weeks.

A bug I caught first

Store IDs were written differently in two sources, which had put 19 shared-warehouse stores in the wrong group. I fixed the join and added a check that must return zero rows before the analysis runs.

Tools
BigQueryContentSquarePythonMann-Whitney test

Client figures anonymized: rates are real, revenue is indexed (standalone = 100, shared warehouse = 118.5), volumes are shown per 1,000 visits.

Case 04 · Dashboards · A global luxury retailer

Dashboards built for leadership

During the holidays, a sales problem found in next week's report is a week too late. I built the view that shows it within the hour, across 21 markets.

Delivered to the client's leadership: published, with a walkthrough prepared for directors and analysts.

  1. The needAre online sales on track today, and against last year, in time to act today?
  2. The rulesSame hours on every comparison. Same weekday last year. The cutoff comes from the data, never the clock.
  3. The buildHistory held in memory, only the last 7 days read live. Fast and still current.
  4. The proofEvery number matched an independent query on the source, revenue to the cent.
  1. Same hours, always. At 2 PM, today is compared with yesterday up to 2 PM, never with all of yesterday.
  2. Today stops at the last complete hour. A half-loaded hour never looks like a crash.
  3. Blank, not minus 100%. A market that is new this year shows no last-year figure instead of a false drop.
42 s → 14 sPage one load time, 3 times faster after one rewrite. Every number stayed identical.
24.6 s → 5.5 sThe slowest query, about 4.5 times faster.

How it was checked

All 16 page-one numbers, every page-two tile and all 21 markets matched an independent query on the source. Against the raw data export, 27 of 27 complete days matched exactly in every market.

What it does not mean

Refreshed every 30 minutes on complete hours is not a live feed. It shows website revenue only, before returns.

Method, checks and tools
Why it is fast

One hour counter sits behind every time window, so "last 24 hours" or "holiday period" is a single range, not a long list of dates and hours.

On the global dashboard

A 12-check test suite must pass before any data load ships. Separately, I caught a shortcut that undercounted one market's active users by 75% to 87%.

Tools
BigQueryGA4Power BIDAXDirectQuery

No client figures are shown. Speeds, counts and percentages are real. The demo's numbers are generated.

Case 05 · Marketing mix modeling · A consumer electronics brand

A readout tool that helped win a project

A marketing mix model tells you what each channel returned. The useful question is different: what will the next dollar return? I built a tool that answers it.

Delivered to the client, as part of a proposal. Outcome: the client was drawn to the tool itself, and the consultancy won the project.

  1. The problemModel results arrive as tables. The people who move the budget cannot read them.
  2. The ideaShow each channel's average return next to the return on its next dollar.
  3. The toolThree pages: results, saturation curves and a budget planner, with a guided tour.
  4. The outcomeIt helped win the project. The model is now being built to feed it.

Try it

The tool runs on illustrative data for Beetles, a made-up audio brand. Open the planner, set a goal, and it recommends a budget split.

Open the demo ↗

What it does not mean

The tool reads a model's results. It is not the model, I did not build the model, and every number in it is illustrative.

Method, checks and tools
The curve

Each channel follows a saturation curve solved from three inputs: current spend, the return at that spend, and the return on the next dollar.

f(x) = K · x^s / (h^s + x^s)

How I checked it

Each curve reproduces its channel's three inputs exactly. The planner reads the same curves as the saturation page, so a budget move and its effect always agree.

Guided tour

One button walks a newcomer through all three pages. It explains one number at a time, spotlights it, and dims the rest.

Tools
JavaScriptHand-drawn charts, no libraryOne self-contained file

Every number in this case is illustrative. No client results are shown.

Also built: a release monitor

A template any teammate can point at a client website. It compares every health metric with three baselines at once. After two tracking launches it flagged a surge of bot traffic, and our team was first to raise it.

What it does not mean
